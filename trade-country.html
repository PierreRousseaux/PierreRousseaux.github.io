Atlas of Trade Dependencies

A country-level extension of the trade-dependency framework, designed to compare import exposures across economies and to study both dependencies on foreign suppliers and the reverse dependencies that other economies have on them.

What the Atlas does

For every economy present in the underlying trade data, the same import-dependency logic as the EU and French pages is run independently: statistically vulnerable products are identified, then weighted by each country's GDP so that a dependency matters more for a small economy than for a large one, with extra weight given to products that sit further upstream in the value chain (a disruption there has more room to cascade). The mirror calculation is run on the export side — how much leverage a country holds over others through its own exports, again GDP-weighted. Dividing the two gives a single openness/vulnerability ratio per country (the DBS: Dependency Balance Score) — a high DBS means a country is structurally more import-dependent than it is export-powerful, a low DBS the reverse.

Why country-level measurement requires care

1
The relevant economic boundary changes. For an EU member state, imports from another member state are foreign in national accounts but remain intra-EU trade. Treating German supply to France exactly like Chinese supply to France would answer a different question from the EU-level diagnostic.
2
Domestic production is essential. The absorption criterion requires product-level production data. Full five-criteria estimates are therefore only possible where sufficiently granular and comparable production data exist; otherwise the Atlas will clearly label the resulting lists as partial or upper-bound diagnostics.
3
Substitution can occur through domestic or foreign suppliers. For a small open economy, the relevant fallback may be production elsewhere in an integrated regional market rather than domestic exports alone. The country methodology therefore has to distinguish national production, regional sourcing and extra-regional sourcing.
4
Re-exports matter. Trade hubs can record very large imports and exports that do not represent domestic final demand. Entrepôt activity must be treated carefully before interpreting trade ratios as national vulnerability.

Planned coverage

The first version will combine CEPII-BACI bilateral HS6 trade data with product-level production data where available. For EU member states, Eurostat Prodcom provides the starting point; the French application can additionally use confidential customs and EAP production microdata. The Atlas will report the exact criteria available for each country rather than pretending that data coverage is identical everywhere.

This page will become the public entry point to the interactive Atlas of Trade Dependencies. Country profiles, product explorers, time-series diagnostics and reverse-dependency indicators will be added once the harmonisation work is complete.
