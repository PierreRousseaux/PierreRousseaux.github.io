French Trade Dependencies

The EU-level diagnostic tells us what is vulnerable. French customs microdata lets us ask a sharper question: how many French firms are actually exposed to those vulnerabilities, and through which countries.

Overview Methodology EU Trade Dependencies French Trade Dependencies Country-Level
A note on scope. This page is deliberately not a re-estimation of the European Commission / Mejean-Rousseaux criteria with France substituted for the EU as the reference economy. Recomputing import concentration, absorption and substitutability using "extra-France" instead of "extra-EU" as the boundary raises serious conceptual problems — see the country-level methodology page for why. Instead, this page keeps the EU-consolidated vulnerability list as given, and asks a different, well-posed question: which French importing firms are exposed to it, using confidential French customs data (CASD).

How exposed are French importing firms to the EU's strategic dependencies?

Linking firm-level French customs records to the EU's 2015–2019 vulnerability lists gives a direct headcount of exposure, at every stage of the funnel:

French firms importing EU strategic-dependency products

Out of all French firms recorded as importing anything, how many import a product on each successive vulnerability list.

Of roughly 241,000 French importing firms, about 70,700 (10.3% of import value) import at least one product on the EC's 3-criteria list, 66,800 (9.0%) import one that also fails the domestic-absorption test, and 17,873 firms (0.5%) import a full 5-criteria strategic dependency. The 17,873 firms represent about 7% of the 241,221 French firms in the customs sample. The 0.5% figure refers instead to the value of French extra-EU imports accounted for by the relevant vulnerable products. Keeping firm incidence and import-value exposure separate is important: the two statistics answer different questions.

How much of that exposure runs through non-NATO countries?

Geopolitical risk is concentrated on the non-NATO side of French trade. Repeating the same firm-count exercise restricted to imports sourced from non-NATO countries:

French firms importing from non-NATO countries

Same population of ~241,000 French importers, restricted to non-NATO sourcing at each stage.

The paper reports that 75% of French extra-EU importers traded with at least one non-NATO country over 2015–2019. Moreover, 24% of French importers imported a product classified as vulnerable under the EC methodology from these countries, compared with 6% under the extended five-criteria classification. These are firm-incidence statistics and should be kept distinct from import-value shares.

These counts describe direct exposure. Mejean and Rousseaux (2024) also use French firm-to-firm intra-EU export data to illustrate indirect exposure. Of 1,453,721 non-French European firms importing from French firms, 1,011,794 (71%) buy from a French firm directly exposed to a product on the EC list, and 652,027 (50%) buy from a French firm exposed to a product on the full five-criteria list. The exercise illustrates how a disruption can propagate beyond the firms that directly import the vulnerable product.

Why look at France specifically?

Three reasons this firm-level French lens adds something the EU-consolidated diagnostic cannot provide on its own:

  • Granularity. Confidential French customs data (HS8, finer than the HS6 used at EU level) and the EAP production survey allow a materially more precise reading of exposure than Eurostat-Prodcom aggregates.
  • Policy relevance. France's own resilience and industrial policy — most directly the France 2030 investment plan (€54bn announced in October 2021, targeting sectors including energy, automotive, aerospace and space) — is evaluated against exactly this kind of firm-level exposure map. Do the firms and value chains France 2030 supports actually sit in structurally vulnerable segments, or are the two only loosely aligned?
  • Propagation. Firm-to-firm linkages mean a dependency entering through one country's importers can still reach firms and consumers across the rest of the EU Single Market — which is why the indirect-exposure figures above matter as much as the direct ones.

France 2030: ongoing evaluation work

This work is part of an ongoing i-MIP research project on the contribution of France 2030 to the resilience of French and European value chains, conducted by Lionel Fontagné (Paris School of Economics & i-MIP) and Pierre Rousseaux (CREST, IPP & i-MIP). The objective is to assess whether France 2030 support is likely to have reduced French trade dependencies and strengthened value-chain resilience. The evaluation combines public CEPII-BACI and Eurostat-Prodcom data with confidential French customs and EAP production microdata accessed through CASD.

The empirical strategy proceeds in three steps:

i
How critical is a given value chain, or a link within it? Applying the same five criteria, combining annual diagnostics over 2015–2023 with pooled estimates over 2015–2019 to distinguish cyclical vulnerabilities from structural dependencies; an additional pooled 2015–2023 exercise is used as a complementary robustness view.
ii
How much overlap is there between France 2030 projects and beneficiaries and the products, services, technologies and actors identified as strategic? This requires matching subsidised firms to their customs and production records to measure coverage and sectoral coherence.
iii
Are the supported actors and value chains aligned with France's and the EU's strategic, technological and economic priorities? The analysis also introduces the value-chain dimension by asking whether supported products use vulnerable inputs themselves, thereby capturing indirect exposure, and compares French priorities with EU-level resilience priorities.

This evaluation work is ongoing; results will be added to this page as they are finalised.
