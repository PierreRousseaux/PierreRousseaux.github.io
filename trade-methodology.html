Trade Dependencies · Methodology

From concentration to strategic vulnerability

A detailed, criterion-by-criterion account of how the recent literature identifies vulnerable products from international trade data — and what Mejean and Rousseaux (2024) add on top of the European Commission's approach.

What exactly is being measured?

The objective is not to create a hierarchy in which “concentration” mechanically becomes “dependency” and then “strategic vulnerability”. The empirical literature instead uses several complementary filters to identify products for which a disruption in foreign supply may be difficult to absorb. Each criterion captures a distinct margin of exposure, and the classifications used on this website are simply the intersections of those criteria.

1
Concentration of extra-EU sourcing. The Herfindahl–Hirschman Index asks whether EU imports of a product are concentrated in a small number of foreign supplier countries. This captures exposure to country-specific shocks, but concentration alone is neither necessary nor sufficient to establish economic vulnerability.
2
Reliance on extra-EU imports. The second Commission criterion asks whether more than half of EU imports of the product originate outside the Union. It therefore measures the importance of third-country sourcing in the EU's import basket.
3
Potential substitution through EU exports. The third Commission criterion compares extra-EU imports with EU exports of the same product. If extra-EU imports exceed total EU exports, observed European export capacity is unlikely to be large enough to replace those imports one-for-one.
4
Domestic production relative to absorption. Mejean and Rousseaux (2024) add production data and ask whether extra-EU imports account for more than half of EU absorption — domestic production + imports − exports. This matters because a product can satisfy the Commission's trade-based criteria while the EU nevertheless produces substantial quantities for its own market.
5
Ex-post supplier substitutability. The final criterion uses relationship stickiness across suppliers to isolate products for which switching source countries after a shock appears particularly difficult. This incorporates a margin that trade shares observed before a shock cannot capture on their own.
The terminology used here follows the underlying exercises: the EC list is the intersection of criteria 1–3; EC + absorption adds criterion 4; and the full five-criteria list adds criterion 5. The extra criteria do not redefine the Commission's concept; they progressively restrict the same product universe by adding domestic-production and supplier-substitution information.

The European Commission's bottom-up approach (Commission Staff Working Document, 2021) is the reference starting point for almost the entire literature. A product is flagged if it simultaneously meets all three conditions: import concentration (HHI > 0.4), a majority extra-EU sourcing share, and extra-EU imports that exceed total EU exports of the same product. All three rely purely on observed trade flows — no production data is needed, which makes the approach easy to replicate across time and countries, but also means it can only proxy for domestic substitution capacity indirectly, through EU export volumes.

Bonneau and Nakaa (2020), for the French Treasury (Direction générale du Trésor), were among the first post-Covid methodologies. They combine an import-origin criterion with an import-concentration criterion (HHI > 0.5) and a network-centrality measure of diversification potential, rather than the EC's export-based substitutability proxy.

Jaravel and Mejean (2021), for the French Council of Economic Analysis (CAE), add a firm-level granularity criterion specific to France: a product is flagged as especially fragile if a single French importing firm accounts for 90% or more of national imports of that product — a check on concentration at the level of individual firms, not just countries.

Baur and Flach (2022), at CESifo, start instead from production relevance: which imported inputs are actually used intensively in the sectors that matter most for the domestic economy. They then apply a somewhat looser concentration threshold (HHI > 0.33) and the same substitutability logic as the EC.

Mejean and Rousseaux (2024) keep the EC's three criteria intact and add two more, aimed specifically at closing the gap between dependency and strategic vulnerability: a domestic-absorption criterion using actual production data (Prodcom-Eurostat) rather than export volumes as the substitution proxy, and an ex-post substitutability criterion built on inter-firm relationship stickiness (Martin et al., 2024). The five Mejean & Rousseaux (2024) criteria, one at a time

Applied at the HS6 product level (2017 nomenclature), using CEPII-BACI trade data and Eurostat-Prodcom production data.

HHI(product) > 0.4

Intuition: if a handful of countries supply almost all of a product, a shock to any one of them cannot easily be absorbed by switching to another supplier in real time.

Data: bilateral trade values by HS6 product and exporting country (CEPII-BACI).

What it eliminates: the vast majority of the roughly 5,000+ HS6 products traded by the EU — most goods are sourced from a reasonably diversified set of countries.

extra-EU imports / total EU imports > 0.5

Intuition: if most of what the EU imports of a product already comes from other member states, the EU has an intra-bloc supply cushion even if the product is concentrated among a few original (possibly non-EU) sources further upstream.

Data: intra-EU vs. extra-EU trade flows (CEPII-BACI).

extra-EU imports / EU exports > 1

Intuition: if the EU exports at least as much of a product as it imports from outside the bloc, that is indirect evidence of a domestic production base large enough to plausibly substitute for the foreign supply.

Limitation this motivates criterion 4: export volumes are only an indirect, and sometimes misleading, proxy for production capacity — round-tripping and re-exports can inflate EU export figures without reflecting genuine local capacity.

extra-EU imports / EU absorption > 0.5, absorption = production + imports − exports

Intuition: complements the export-based proxy in criterion 3 with actual EU production data. A product only qualifies if extra-EU imports account for more than half of EU absorption, defined as production plus imports minus exports. This criterion directly asks whether foreign supply is quantitatively important relative to the amount of the product absorbed in the EU.

Data: Eurostat-Prodcom production statistics, matched to HS6 trade codes.

What it eliminates: products for which the EU already produces enough to cover most of its own needs even though import flows look concentrated — for example, many agricultural products protected by the Common Agricultural Policy. This single criterion cuts the EC-list roughly in half in the 2015–2019 reference period.

relationship stickiness (Martin et al.) in the top quartile of the HS6 distribution

Intuition: even a highly concentrated, poorly substitutable-by-production product may not be a genuine vulnerability if buyers can quickly switch to a different foreign supplier after a shock. The stickiness indicator is a product-level measure derived from the persistence of firm-to-firm trade relationships. Products with especially persistent relationships are interpreted as having lower ex-post supplier substitutability: when a relationship is disrupted, finding and qualifying an alternative supplier is likely to be more difficult. Mejean and Rousseaux retain products in the upper quartile of the HS6 stickiness distribution (a value above 3.2 in their application).

Why this matters empirically: Boehm, Flaaen and Pandalai-Nayar (2019) find that Japanese firms hit by the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake cut production almost one-for-one with the drop in imports — consistent with rigid, Leontief-like input structures. But Lafrogne-Joussier, Martin and Mejean (2023) find that French firms exposed to the first China lockdown in January 2020 fared no worse if they were undiversified beforehand, because undiversified firms were also the fastest to find new foreign partners after the shock. Ex-ante diversification and ex-post substitutability do not always move together, which is exactly why criterion 5 is measured directly rather than assumed.

Related literature

The five-criteria framework builds on a broader body of work on global value chains, shock transmission, and resilience. The synthesis below (Rousseaux, 2025) summarises the papers most directly relevant to why concentration, absorption and substitutability are the right things to measure.

Proposes an operational measure of a product's average distance from final demand, built as a fixed-point solution over the input-output matrix (related to the Leontief inverse). Intermediate-goods sectors — chemicals, base metals, some capital goods — have upstreamness routinely above 3; sectors selling directly to households sit near 1. This is the measure used throughout this section to flag products whose disruption would cascade through several further stages of production before reaching consumers.
A conceptual synthesis linking property-rights theory, incomplete contracts and vertical organisation to the structure of global value chains. Institutional and political risk shape which tasks end up where in a chain; the paper's central message for resilience policy is that any intervention has to trade off the static efficiency gains of fragmented, specialised production against the new vulnerabilities that fragmentation itself creates.
Proposes a taxonomy of supply-chain risk (natural, economic, geoeconomic, policy-driven) and argues that global value chains did not "fail" during Covid so much as reveal specific, identifiable weak points. The policy implication carried into this framework: target diversification and buffers at specific critical links rather than pursuing indiscriminate reshoring.
Uses the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake and detailed US firm-import data to show that American firms more exposed to Japanese suppliers in the earthquake-affected region cut production almost one-for-one with the fall in imports, and that the effect persisted for several quarters — evidence of rigid, low-substitutability input structures exactly where inputs are specific and hard to replace quickly.
A multi-country, multi-sector general-equilibrium model calibrated to pre-pandemic input-output linkages, used to run counterfactuals against a world with shorter or more domestic supply chains. Finds that existing GVC structure somewhat amplified the fall in global GDP relative to a less fragmented counterfactual, but the effect is heterogeneous across countries — the question is not whether to use GVCs, but which GVC structure best withstands shocks.
Shows that news coverage of shocks accelerates and amplifies how quickly firms reroute supply chains, because information frictions — not just physical constraints — slow down supplier switching. This motivates a policy role for public data and transparency on trade exposure as a complement to subsidies: better information is itself a resilience tool.
Using French customs data linking exporters to foreign buyers, shows that a substantial share of international business-cycle comovement is "granular" — driven by a small number of very large firm-to-firm trade relationships rather than diffuse aggregate exposure. The 2020 follow-up shows the same logic applies to how foreign shocks propagate into an importing economy: concentrated supplier relationships transmit shocks disproportionately. This is a direct empirical justification for looking at concentration among suppliers, not just aggregate trade shares.
A theoretical model of firms choosing a portfolio of domestic and foreign suppliers under fixed relationship costs and disruption risk. In most parameterisations, well-designed international diversification reduces disruption risk more efficiently than reshoring to a single domestic supplier, which merely swaps foreign concentration risk for domestic concentration risk while sacrificing specialisation gains.
Models resilience along a multi-stage vertical chain where firms can add redundant suppliers at each stage at a cost. Optimal resilience is not uniform along the chain: some links (typically the most upstream or most critical) justify heavy redundancy, others do not — supporting a targeted, not blanket, approach to identifying which links deserve policy attention.
The Covid-era counterpart to Boehm et al. (2019): using French firm-level import and production data around the first China lockdown (January 2020), the paper finds no significant difference in outcomes between firms that were diversified beforehand and those that were not — because previously undiversified firms were also the fastest to find new foreign suppliers after the shock. This is the direct empirical motivation for measuring ex-post substitutability explicitly, rather than assuming it from ex-ante diversification, which is exactly what the stickiness-based criterion 5 does.
Evaluates, after the fact, how much substitution capacity (new suppliers, demand reduction, input substitution) cushioned the German economy against the loss of Russian gas following the invasion of Ukraine. Realised GDP losses were far smaller than initial worst-case projections assuming no substitution — a clean illustration that vulnerability depends as much on substitutability as on the initial level of dependence, the central logic behind criterion 5.
