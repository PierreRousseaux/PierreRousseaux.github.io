Trade Dependencies

Identifying, quantifying, and weighting the European Union's imported trade dependencies — and turning that diagnostic into a usable input for resilience policy, for the European Union, for France, and (soon) for individual member states.

CEPR & Bruegel — Paris Report 2 (2024) EIB Investment Report 2024/2025 i-MIP · France 2030 evaluation
Overview Methodology EU Trade Dependencies French Trade Dependencies Country-Level

Global value chains let firms and consumers capture large gains from specialisation — but they also concentrate production in a small number of nodes. When one of those nodes is disrupted, the shock can propagate through entire downstream industries. This section maps out where the European Union's economy is exposed to that kind of concentrated, hard-to-substitute foreign sourcing, how that exposure has evolved since 2002, what it looks like for France specifically, and how the same logic can — carefully — be pushed down to the level of individual member states.

Why does this matter?

Global value chains (GVCs) are complex networks that coordinate the production of a good from inception to final purchase, linking buyers and suppliers across many stages and countries. Over the last three decades, GVCs have become far more geographically dispersed. This hyper-globalisation has raised trade gains for countries, firms and consumers, and offers a degree of risk-sharing against country-specific shocks (Backus et al., 1992; Antràs and Chor, 2021). But it has also concentrated production within specific nodes, heightening exposure to local supply shocks — disruptions at one node can propagate downstream through entire industries (di Giovanni et al., 2020; Bonadio et al., 2021; Boehm et al., 2019).

The risk attached to GVC nodes that rely heavily on a handful of firms and countries has sparked a policy debate about global trade dependencies. The Covid-19 pandemic and Russia's invasion of Ukraine exposed how reliant economies are on a small number of countries for pharmaceuticals and other critical goods, pushing strategic autonomy and supply-chain resilience up the policy agenda. Identifying these vulnerabilities — and separating the ones that matter from the much larger number of goods that are merely concentrated — is a necessary first step for designing effective resilience policy.

Resilience policy has to balance the benefits of GVC concentration and hyper-globalisation against reduced exposure to risks such as trade weaponisation, critical-goods shortages, supply-chain disruption and lost competitiveness. Recent measures — the US's 2021 Executive Order on America's Supply Chains, the European Chips Act — try to mitigate concentrated-GVC risk through diversification or local production. Public intervention is also justified on efficiency grounds: firms under-invest in resilience because of network and information externalities that they cannot fully internalise on their own.

The same diagnostic is useful whenever trade relations become a source of economic or geopolitical risk. It can identify where the EU relies on a narrow set of foreign suppliers and, symmetrically, where foreign economies rely on European supply. These are descriptive measures of exposure, not by themselves a measure of bargaining power: the economic consequences of a disruption also depend on substitution possibilities, the position of the product in value chains, inventories and the availability of alternative technologies.

The core empirical challenge is not listing every product the EU imports from outside its borders — most of that trade is entirely unproblematic. It is to distinguish products that look concentrated in trade data from exposures for which concentration is compounded by limited European production and low scope for supplier substitution. The five-criteria framework is deliberately restrictive: it is designed to isolate a small set of acute vulnerabilities, not to label all concentrated imports as strategic dependencies. The methodology page walks through exactly how that separation is done.

Where this comes from

This section is built on a specific, published research programme rather than a one-off exercise:

  • Mejean and Rousseaux (2024), Identifying European Trade Dependencies, the third chapter of Paris Report 2: Europe's Economic Security (CEPR & Bruegel, May 2024), edited by J. Pisani-Ferry, B. Weder di Mauro and J. Zettelmeyer. This is the main methodological and empirical reference for the whole section.
  • A contribution to the European Investment Bank's Investment Report 2024/2025 (Chapter 5, Box C), extending the diagnostic back to 2002 and forward through the Covid period.
  • Ongoing work as part of the i-MIP evaluation of the France 2030 investment plan, where the same diagnostic is being pushed down to the level of individual value chains and, eventually, individual beneficiary firms, to assess whether public support is actually targeted at genuinely critical dependencies.
  • A dedicated exploitation of French customs microdata (CASD) to characterise which French importing firms are actually exposed to the EU's strategic dependencies, and how that exposure runs through non-NATO countries specifically.
