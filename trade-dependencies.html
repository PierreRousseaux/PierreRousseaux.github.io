Trade Dependencies

Globalisation has made production more efficient — but also more interconnected. This research programme identifies where those interdependencies become vulnerabilities, evaluates the risks they create, and studies which policies can strengthen resilience without unnecessarily giving up the gains from trade.

THE QUESTION

When does interdependence become a risk?

Over the past three decades, production has become increasingly fragmented across countries. Firms organise production through complex global value chains (GVCs), locating extraction, processing, component production, assembly and distribution across different economies. This international specialisation has generated substantial gains from trade: lower production costs, greater product variety, access to specialised inputs and technologies, and opportunities to share country-specific risks across borders (Backus et al., 1992; Antràs and Chor, 2021).

But the same forces that have made global production more efficient have also created an unprecedented network of interdependencies. Production of some inputs has become concentrated in a small number of firms, countries or nodes of global production networks. When these inputs are difficult to replace, a disruption affecting one supplier can propagate through downstream industries and eventually generate aggregate economic effects (di Giovanni and Levchenko, 2010, 2012; di Giovanni et al., 2020; Bonadio et al., 2021).

GLOBAL VALUE CHAINS

How did trade dependencies emerge?

Trade dependencies are not an anomaly of globalisation. They are partly the consequence of the same mechanisms that generate its benefits.

Falling transport and communication costs, trade liberalisation and the integration of new production centres into the world economy have allowed firms to separate geographically the different stages of production. Countries increasingly specialise not only in industries, but in tasks: extraction, refining, component manufacturing, assembly, logistics, design or services.

Such specialisation can generate economies of scale, productivity gains and lower prices. International trade can also provide insurance against domestic shocks: when production is disrupted in one economy, foreign production may substitute for it.

Yet specialisation can simultaneously concentrate production in particular locations. A product may ultimately depend on only a handful of producers, even when the final supply chain involves firms located across many countries.

This is particularly important when inputs are highly specific. Replacing a supplier may require finding a producer with the appropriate technology, testing and certifying its output, adapting production lines, renegotiating contracts and reorganising logistics.

Supplier relationships can therefore be remarkably persistent. The relevant question is not simply how geographically diversified a supply chain appears, but how easily it can actually adjust when one of its nodes disappears.

WHY IT MATTERS

From efficient networks to economic vulnerability

Recent shocks have made this trade-off particularly visible. The Covid-19 pandemic disrupted international transport and access to medical supplies and intermediate inputs. Subsequent shortages of semiconductors constrained production far beyond the electronics industry. Russia's invasion of Ukraine then demonstrated how an existing commercial dependency could rapidly become an economic and geopolitical vulnerability.

Export restrictions, sanctions, geopolitical tensions, disruptions to maritime routes and renewed tariff conflicts have since placed supply-chain resilience and economic security at the centre of policy debates.

A CRUCIAL DISTINCTION

Foreign sourcing is not, by itself, a vulnerability.

Modern economies import thousands of products. Trying to eliminate interdependence would be neither realistic nor economically desirable.

01

What?

Which products or inputs does the economy rely on?

02

From whom?

How concentrated are imports across foreign suppliers?

03

Can we substitute?

Could domestic production or alternative foreign suppliers replace disrupted imports?

04

What happens if supply stops?

Would the disruption affect consumers, essential goods, downstream production or strategically important technologies?

The objective is therefore not to produce the longest possible list of foreign dependencies. It is to isolate the relatively small set of dependencies for which disruption would be both difficult to absorb and economically or socially costly.

FROM PRIVATE CHOICES TO PUBLIC POLICY

Why might markets provide too little resilience?

Firms already have strong incentives to protect themselves against supply disruptions. But the resilience chosen privately by each firm need not coincide with the resilience that is desirable for the economy as a whole.

01

Network externalities

In a production network, the consequences of a firm's disruption are not necessarily confined to that firm. Its customers — and potentially their own customers — may also be affected. Investments in resilience can therefore generate benefits elsewhere in the network that are not fully internalised by the firm making the investment.

Grossman et al. (2021, 2023)
02

Information frictions

Firms generally know their direct suppliers much better than the suppliers of their suppliers. A seemingly domestic or European input may therefore conceal indirect exposure to a highly concentrated foreign source further upstream in the production network.

Bui et al. (2022)
03

Private versus social costs

For essential goods, the social consequences of a shortage can be much larger than the private loss incurred by an individual importer. Society may consequently place a different value on insurance against rare but costly disruptions.

Baldwin & Freeman (2021)
THE RESEARCH APPROACH

Diagnose first. Assess the risk. Then choose the policy.

Resilience policy should start from a granular diagnosis rather than from a presumption that foreign sourcing is undesirable.

1
IDENTIFY

Map trade dependencies

Use detailed trade and production data to identify products for which sourcing is concentrated, reliance on foreign supply is substantial, domestic production provides limited fallback capacity and alternative suppliers may be difficult to mobilise.

→
2
QUALIFY

Identify the underlying risk

A dependency matters because of the consequences of its disruption. These consequences can differ substantially across products and across risks.

3
RESPOND

Match instruments to risks

Diversification, inventories, information provision, standardisation, strategic reserves, industrial policy or domestic capacity do not solve the same problem. The appropriate instrument depends on the vulnerability and the risk being insured against.

THE BROADER QUESTION

How much resilience should we buy?

Resilience is not free. Diversifying suppliers, maintaining inventories, duplicating capacity or subsidising domestic production can provide insurance against disruptions, but they can also reduce some of the gains generated by specialisation.

The objective of economic-security policy should therefore not be maximum self-sufficiency. It should be to identify the risks society wants to insure against, determine where private incentives are insufficient, and choose the least costly instrument capable of delivering the desired level of resilience.
