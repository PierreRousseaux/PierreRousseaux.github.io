EU trade dependencies

What can you find on this page?

This page is mainly based on the paper Mejean and Rousseaux (2024), which is the third chapter of the Paris Report 2 by CEPR and Bruegel, titled Europe's Economic Security, published in May 2024 and edited by Pisani-Ferry, J., B. Weder di Mauro, and J. Zettelmeyer. It also includes results from a contribution into EIB (European Investment Bank)'s Investment Report 2024/2025 (Chapter 5, Box C).

This page therefore aims to explain the utility of statistical methodologies employed to analyze and identify imported trade vulnerabilities and to describe the findings of our paper: What are the imported trade vulnerabilities in Europe? How do they evolve over time? What are some examples of vulnerable products? What is their sectoral distribution?

Once these vulnerabilities are identified, not all products pose the same risks to the European economy. In Mejean and Rousseaux (2024), we cross these vulnerabilities with four non-exhaustive normative arguments:

  1. Products posing high geopolitical risks, such as tariff wars or the recent Ukrainian crisis.
  2. Products with strong supply chain risks, affecting downstream industries in global value chains all the way to consumers (e.g., semiconductors).
  3. Products posing critical risks, such as pharmaceuticals, particularly their active pharmaceutical ingredients.
  4. Products in emerging cutting-edge technologies, particularly in the green industry, where Europe must enhance its resilience.

I finally provide a detailed discussion of the public policies presented in our contribution to increase Europe's resilience. Incoming, I also develop a refinement of the paper by breaking down our methodology at the country level within the European Union.

Why should we pay attention to and quantify the EU's imported trade dependencies?

Global value chains (GVCs) are complex networks that coordinate the production of goods from inception to consumer purchase, involving intricate links between buyers and suppliers across various stages. Over the last three decades, GVCs have undergone significant hyper-globalization, leading to highly dispersed geographical production.

This fragmentation has increased trade gains for countries, firms, and consumers, offering risk-sharing properties against country-specific shocks. However, it has also led to increased concentration of production within specific nodes, heightening exposure to local supply shocks, as disruptions in one node can affect downstream industries.

The Covid-19 pandemic and the Russian invasion of Ukraine highlighted economies' significant reliance on a few countries for pharmaceuticals and critical goods, stressing the need for strategic autonomy and supply chain resilience. Identifying these vulnerabilities and isolating them according to their risks is crucial for designing effective resilience policies. Public intervention can also be justified by potential underinvestment in resilience by firms because of network and information externalities.

This issue is also crucial in the context of US tariffs on Europe: identifying EU dependencies on the US can inform diversification strategies and support for local production. The methodology can also be applied in reverse to identify strategic dependencies of other countries on the EU and potential trade-response levers.

How can we identify and quantify these trade dependencies?

Several methodologies emerged following the Covid-19 pandemic, including those of the French Treasury, the French Council of Economic Advisors, the European Commission, Mejean and Rousseaux (2024), and CESifo. The European Commission methodology identifies a product as vulnerable if it simultaneously meets criteria for import concentration, the significance of extra-EU imports, and the substitutability of these imports with domestic exports.

In Mejean and Rousseaux (2024), we add potential domestic and post-shock substitution, focusing on products mainly reliant on extra-EU imports to meet domestic demand and with very low supplier substitution potential. We use product-level trade (CEPII-BACI) and production (Eurostat-PRODCOM) data at the HS6 2017 nomenclature.

Recap of the five criteria developed in Mejean and Rousseaux (2024)
  1. Concentration of EU imports: Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) > 0.4.
  2. Importance of extra-EU imports in total EU demand: extra-EU imports / total EU imports > 0.5.
  3. Substitutability of extra-EU imports by EU exports: extra-EU imports / total EU exports > 1.
  4. Low domestic EU production relative to EU demand: extra-EU imports / EU absorption > 0.5.
  5. Low substitutability between suppliers: relationship stickiness in the top 25% of the HS6 product distribution.

How many trade vulnerabilities are imported from the EU?

Beginning with 5,381 products imported by the consolidated EU over 2015–2019, the European Commission's three criteria identify 378 vulnerable products. Restricting the set to products for which 50% of domestic absorption is satisfied by extra-EU imports reduces it to 228 products. Finally, considering ex-post substitutability leaves 49 strategic dependencies. This last set accounts for 0.5% of total EU imports and is notably concentrated in energy, mining, basic metals and chemicals. Most of these strategic dependencies are associated with products primarily sourced from China.

What about their evolution over a longer time frame?

Reproducing the analysis from 2002 onward gives sets of vulnerabilities identified by the intersection of the three EC criteria, then four criteria, and finally all five criteria. The analysis separates the pre-Global Financial Crisis period, the post-GFC period, the recovery period and the Covid period. A clear before-and-after effect surrounds the financial crisis, with a plateau emerging regardless of methodology. Five-criteria vulnerabilities account for roughly 0.1% to 1% of EU import volume, compared with 2.9% to 5.4% under broader methodologies.

Vulnerabilities tend to persist over time: 41% of dependencies identified before the global financial crisis persisted directly afterwards and 35% remained after that when applying all five criteria. Between 40% and 54% of vulnerabilities identified with broader methodologies have EU production that mainly satisfies domestic demand; after adding criterion (iv), 77% to 80% can easily be substituted using different suppliers.

What is the nature of these products?

Products identified as vulnerable over 2015–2019 include alkaloids, almost entirely imported from the United Kingdom (96% of EU imports; HHI 0.92); quebracho extract from Argentina (96%; HHI 0.92); artificial flowers and plastic foliage from China (95%; HHI 0.90); and trichloroethylene from the United States (90%; HHI 0.82).

Other examples include synthetic-fibre tents and cases or containers predominantly imported from China (80%; HHI 0.65), iodine from Chile (73%; HHI 0.56), raw beryllium from the United States (62%; HHI 0.52), and bismuth from China (62%; HHI 0.41). The consequences of shortages differ greatly across these products, which motivates weighting statistical vulnerabilities by their economic, geopolitical and human consequences.

The vulnerable goods are predominantly imported from China, followed by the United States and/or India. Adding domestic production and ex-post substitution substantially changes their sectoral distribution. Around 70% of strategic dependencies are concentrated in textile, construction, energy and mining, and health. In agriculture, the number falls from 42 to 13 products after applying the domestic-absorption criterion.

Four normative risk dimensions

1 — Geopolitical risk

Between 2015 and 2019, 70–80% of identified trade vulnerabilities were primarily sourced from non-NATO countries. Linking these dependencies to the Geopolitical Risk Index shows that several strategic dependencies imported from non-NATO countries are exposed to above-median geopolitical risk.

Examples include quebracho extract from Argentina, lithium carbonate and iodine from Chile, vulcanised-rubber gloves from Malaysia, and refractory clay from Ukraine. Trade dependencies are also increasingly associated with Chinese exports: China's share in EU import vulnerabilities is rising, while the shares of the United States and the rest of the world have declined.

2 — Supply chain risks

Shocks can propagate downstream through global value chains. To assess this dimension, strategic dependencies are cross-referenced with their upstreamness — the average number of production stages before final consumption — and with end-use classifications.

The analysis identifies 22 products more than three stages away from final consumers. Highly upstream vulnerable products are concentrated in energy, mining, basic metals and chemicals and are predominantly imported from China. Examples include acyclic hydrocarbons from Russia (upstreamness 4.20), trichloroethylene from the United States (3.85), quebracho extract from Argentina (3.85), raw beryllium from the United States (3.42), and magnesium products from China (3.40). Highly upstream products represent around 49% of five-criteria vulnerabilities on average across periods.

3 — Risk of shortages of critical goods

Shortages of critical goods can generate severe non-economic consequences and justify public intervention. The pharmaceutical sector is especially important. Linking trade categories to active pharmaceutical ingredients connects 170 HS6 product categories to 4,497 APIs.

Daprodustat, used to treat anaemia in chronic kidney disease, and metharbital, used to treat epilepsy, emerge as particularly critical. Both are primarily imported from India, with market shares exceeding 97% and an HHI of 0.94. An additional 136 APIs are identified under a broader four-criteria approach, including pregabalin, atrumustine and bimatoprost.

4 — Risk to competitiveness in future key technologies

A forward-looking perspective is essential for green technologies. Lithium-ion batteries, solar panels, hydrogen-related products and rare-earth metals combine supplier concentration, geopolitical exposure and technological substitution possibilities.

Lithium carbonate is concentrated around Chile and Argentina; cobalt ore and concentrates are overwhelmingly supplied by the Democratic Republic of Congo. Solar-panel supply chains are heavily exposed to China and other Asian suppliers. Hydrogen markets are more diversified, while rare-earth markets remain strategically concentrated. These patterns underline the need for proactive diversification and technological innovation.

What public policies to use to improve resilience?

The policy toolbox developed in Mejean and Rousseaux (2024) combines subsidies, regulatory frameworks and industrial policies. Subsidies can target domestic production, R&D or resilience investments at firm level. Firms can enhance resilience through supply-chain visibility, redundancy, agility and vertical integration, but these investments may be underfunded because of network and information externalities.

The EU can use granular datasets to provide firms with better information about trade exposures. Real-time monitoring systems, such as France's drug-stock tracking initiative, illustrate how governments can enhance visibility in critical sectors. Coordination and standardisation can also reduce vulnerabilities: harmonising product designs and architectures makes production across multiple suppliers and facilities easier.

Tax and subsidy frameworks can encourage agility and diversified supplier relationships. In critical sectors, strategic reserves or domestic production capacity may be warranted. Resilience policy must also anticipate future needs in green technologies: diversification toward low-risk suppliers and technological alternatives can reduce reliance on highly concentrated lithium, cobalt and rare-earth markets.

At EU level, coordination is essential to prevent subsidy wars between Member States. Although industrial policy is largely a Member State competence, resilience investments benefit the Union as a whole and therefore have a public-good dimension. A comprehensive EU framework combining subsidies, data-driven information, standardisation and coordinated investment can strengthen economic security while preserving the gains from trade.
