EU trade dependencies

This page is mainly based on Mejean and Rousseaux (2024), the third chapter of CEPR and Bruegel's Paris Report 2: Europe's Economic Security, and also includes results from the EIB Investment Report 2024/2025 (Chapter 5, Box C).

Why should we pay attention to the EU's imported trade dependencies?

Global value chains combine the gains from international specialisation with exposure to disruptions at concentrated production nodes. The Covid-19 pandemic and Russia's invasion of Ukraine highlighted economies' reliance on a small number of countries for pharmaceuticals, critical goods and industrial inputs. Identifying these vulnerabilities is therefore a prerequisite for designing resilience policies that preserve the gains from trade while reducing exposure to severe shocks.

How can we identify and quantify these dependencies?

The European Commission methodology identifies a product as vulnerable when imports are concentrated, extra-EU imports are important, and these imports cannot readily be substituted by EU exports. Mejean and Rousseaux (2024) add two criteria capturing domestic production capacity and post-shock substitution across suppliers.

Five criteria
  1. Concentration of EU imports: HHI > 0.4.
  2. Extra-EU imports / total EU imports > 0.5.
  3. Extra-EU imports / total EU exports > 1.
  4. Extra-EU imports / EU absorption > 0.5.
  5. Relationship stickiness in the top 25% of the HS6 product distribution.

How many vulnerabilities?

Among 5,381 products imported by the consolidated EU in 2015–2019, the European Commission's three criteria identify 378 vulnerable products. Adding the domestic-absorption criterion reduces this to 228; imposing low supplier substitutability leaves 49 strategic dependencies, accounting for about 0.5% of total EU imports. These products are concentrated in energy, mining, basic metals and chemicals, and many are primarily sourced from China.

Evolution over time

Applying the methodologies over 2002–2020 shows that the narrowest five-criteria measure represents only a small share of EU imports. Dependencies are nevertheless persistent: 41% of those identified before the global financial crisis persisted directly afterwards, and 35% remained in the following period.

What is the nature of these products?

Examples include alkaloids, quebracho extract, trichloroethylene, iodine, raw beryllium and bismuth. Their strategic importance differs sharply, illustrating why statistical vulnerability measures need to be combined with information on the economic and geopolitical consequences of disruption.

Four risk dimensions

1. Geopolitical risk

A large share of identified vulnerabilities is sourced from non-NATO countries. China has become increasingly prominent among EU import vulnerabilities, while the shares of the United States and the rest of the world have declined.

2. Supply-chain risks

Upstream inputs can propagate shocks through multiple downstream industries. The analysis identifies 22 vulnerable products located more than three production stages from final consumers; examples include acyclic hydrocarbons, trichloroethylene, quebracho extract, beryllium and magnesium.

3. Shortages of critical goods

The pharmaceutical sector is particularly important. Linking trade categories to active pharmaceutical ingredients reveals highly concentrated dependencies for some APIs, including products mainly imported from India, Singapore and the United States.

4. Future key technologies

Green technologies require a forward-looking view of resilience. Lithium-ion batteries, solar panels, hydrogen-related products and rare-earth materials combine varying degrees of supplier concentration and geopolitical exposure, making diversification and technological substitution central policy questions.

What public policies can improve resilience?

The policy toolbox includes subsidies, regulation and industrial policy. Firms can invest in supply-chain visibility, redundancy, agility and vertical integration, but network and information externalities may lead to underinvestment. Public policy can improve granular information, encourage standardisation and supplier diversification, support strategic reserves and domestic capacity in critical sectors, and coordinate resilience investments at the EU level to avoid subsidy races between Member States.
