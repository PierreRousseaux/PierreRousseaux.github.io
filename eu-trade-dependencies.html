EU Trade Dependencies

What are Europe's imported trade vulnerabilities, how can we identify them, what risks do they pose, and which policies can strengthen resilience?

This page is based mainly on Méjean and Rousseaux (2024), Chapter 3 of the CEPR–Bruegel Paris Report 2, Europe's Economic Security, and on a contribution to the EIB Investment Report 2024/2025 (Chapter 5, Box C).

Why should we pay attention to trade dependencies?

Global value chains have generated substantial gains from specialisation and international risk sharing, but they can also concentrate production in particular firms, products and countries. Local disruptions can therefore propagate through downstream industries. The COVID-19 pandemic and Russia's invasion of Ukraine brought these vulnerabilities to the centre of debates on strategic autonomy and supply-chain resilience.

The policy challenge is not to eliminate international interdependence. It is to identify vulnerabilities that can generate particularly costly disruptions, distinguish the risks they create, and choose resilience policies whose insurance benefits justify their costs.

How can we identify trade dependencies?

The European Commission's bottom-up approach identifies vulnerable products using import concentration, reliance on extra-EU imports and limited substitutability through EU exports. Méjean and Rousseaux (2024) add two dimensions: the ability of EU production to satisfy domestic demand and the potential to substitute across suppliers after a shock.

The final set represents about 0.5% of EU imports in the pooled 2015–2019 analysis and is concentrated particularly in energy, mining, basic metals and chemicals.

How have dependencies evolved?

Extending the exercise over 2002–2022 shows a rise around the global financial crisis followed by a plateau, with another change around the COVID period. Under the most restrictive five-criteria methodology, acute vulnerabilities account for only a small fraction of EU trade, but they are persistent: 41% of dependencies identified before the global financial crisis persisted directly afterwards, and 35% remained in the following period.

What kinds of products are vulnerable?

The products range from relatively low-stakes consumer goods to inputs with potentially systemic consequences. Examples in the 2015–2019 analysis include quebracho extract from Argentina, trichloroethylene from the United States, iodine from Chile, raw beryllium from the United States and bismuth from China.

This heterogeneity is crucial. A statistical dependency is not automatically a strategic risk: disruption to artificial flowers or suitcases does not have the same consequences as disruption to beryllium, pharmaceutical ingredients or upstream chemical inputs. The methodology therefore needs to be combined with explicit risk dimensions.

Four dimensions of strategic risk

1. Geopolitical risk

A large share of identified vulnerabilities is sourced from non-NATO countries, and China's role in EU import vulnerabilities has increased over time. Combining dependency measures with geopolitical-risk indicators helps distinguish concentrated trade relationships that may be especially exposed to coercion, conflict or trade restrictions.

2. Supply-chain risk

Upstream products can transmit shocks through multiple downstream production stages. In the five-criteria set, 22 products are more than three stages from final consumers, and highly upstream dependencies are concentrated in energy, mining, basic metals and chemicals. Across periods, roughly half of the five-criteria vulnerabilities are highly upstream.

3. Critical-goods shortages

For pharmaceuticals, shortages can generate social costs far beyond lost output. Linking trade categories to active pharmaceutical ingredients identifies particularly concentrated dependencies, including APIs primarily sourced from India. This provides a concrete case where private resilience incentives and societal insurance needs may diverge.

4. Future technologies and competitiveness

Resilience also has a forward-looking dimension. Lithium-ion batteries, solar technologies, hydrogen and rare-earth inputs illustrate how supplier concentration can matter for Europe's future competitiveness even when the relevant products are not yet classified as current strategic dependencies.

What policies can improve resilience?

The appropriate toolbox depends on the source of the risk. Policies can support domestic production, R&D and firm-level resilience investments; improve supply-chain visibility and information; promote standardisation and substitutability; create strategic reserves for essential goods; and coordinate industrial investment at EU level.

Information and network externalities provide an important rationale for intervention: firms may know their direct suppliers but have limited visibility further upstream, while an upstream firm's resilience investment can benefit many downstream firms. Public data infrastructure, monitoring and coordination can therefore complement private diversification.

The central principle: resilience policy should be targeted. The objective is not autarky, but to insure the European economy against disruptions where concentration, weak substitution and the economic or societal cost of shortages jointly make the exposure strategic.
